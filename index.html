Topic One
The Human Experience
On living, feeling, and what it means to exist in this strange, beautiful world.
A note before you read: These are the pieces I kept thinking about when I thought about you — the kind of writing that holds a mirror up and says, "yes, this is what being alive feels like." I hope they feel like company. 🌿
Essay · Seneca · 49 AD
On the Shortness of Life — and What We Do With It
Lucius Annaeus Seneca · De Brevitate Vitae

It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it. Life is long enough, and a sufficiently generous amount has been given to us for the highest achievements if it were all well invested. But when it is wasted in heedless luxury and spent on no good activity, we are forced at last by death's final constraint to realize that it has passed away before we knew it was passing.

"So it is — the life we receive is not short, but we make it so; we are not ill provided but use what we have wastefully."

— Seneca, De Brevitate Vitae

Everyone hustles his life along, and is troubled by a longing for the future and a weariness of the present. But the man who spends all his time on his own needs, who organizes every day as though it were his last, neither longs for nor fears the next day. What can give any man pleasure that he is not enjoying right now? Nothing — for we are always, always waiting for the real living to begin.

✦

On busyness — and how it steals us from ourselves

There is nothing the busy man is less busied with than living: there is nothing that is harder to learn. Reflect, then, on whether devoted men and women of ambition, who drag out their lives among meaningless tasks, are not in the same position as that person who spent every waking hour writing, dictating, listening, speaking — and never had time to live, only to exist at high speed.

Of all people, only those are at leisure who make time for philosophy — only those are really alive. They not only keep a good watch over their own lifetimes, but they annex every age to their own. All the years that have gone before are added to theirs.

"Omnia, Lucili, aliena sunt, tempus tantum nostrum est."
Everything, Lucilius, belongs to others; time alone is ours.

— Seneca, Letters

The present moment always will have been. That is perhaps the strangest, most comforting thought Seneca gives us: whatever we live fully — truly, completely — cannot be taken away. It happened. It is permanent now. The only question is whether we will let enough of life happen to us before it ends.

Long Read · Oliver Burkeman · 2021
Why Time Feels Like It's Speeding Up — and How to Slow It Down
Oliver Burkeman · Four Thousand Weeks

There's a phenomenon that almost everyone notices, usually sometime in their thirties, that time seems to be accelerating. The years blur. Summers that once felt eternal now vanish in weeks. And the strange thing is that this isn't an illusion — it's a mathematical and psychological inevitability, baked into what it means to be a creature who experiences time.

When you are ten years old, a year is one tenth of your entire existence. It looms large, stretches wide. When you are forty, that same year is one fortieth of your life — proportionally tiny. This is the first, ruthlessly logical reason that childhood felt so vast and adulthood feels so compressed.

"The most radical possible response to the brevity of life isn't to try to do more — it's to let yourself be more fully present in what you're already doing."

— Oliver Burkeman, Four Thousand Weeks
✦

Novelty is time's secret ingredient

The brain doesn't record time in clock-ticks — it records memories, and memories are disproportionately formed during novel experiences. When everything is new, the brain is frantically taking notes. It lays down dense, detailed memory. When you look back, it feels long. Routine, by contrast, is nearly invisible to memory. A Wednesday in February, doing what you always do, leaves almost no trace.

The implication is almost too simple: the way to slow time is not to become more efficient. It is to do things for the first time. Every genuine novelty is the brain saying, this matters, I'm writing this down.

"A life of only familiar things is a life that memory barely notices. And a life that memory barely notices seems, looking back, to have barely happened."

— Burkeman

Presence — actual, phone-away, eyes-open presence — also thickens time. Not because the clock slows, but because you are actually inhabiting the moment instead of being halfway somewhere else. Most of us, most of the time, are somewhere else. The moment passes unoccupied. None of this is easy. But it is available. Right now, actually.

Personal Essay · James Baldwin · 1962
A Letter to My Nephew — On Being Human in a World That Forgets You Are
James Baldwin · The Fire Next Time, 1962

I have begun this letter five times and torn it up five times. I keep seeing your face, which is also the face of your father and my brother. You were born where you were born and faced the future that you faced because you were black and for no other reason. The limits of your ambition were, thus, expected to be set forever.

"Please try to remember that what they believe, as well as what they do and cause you to endure, does not testify to your inferiority but to their inhumanity and fear."

— James Baldwin, The Fire Next Time
✦

What it means to know who you are

This is the crime of which I accuse my country and my countrymen — that they have destroyed and are destroying hundreds of thousands of lives and do not know it and do not want to know it. But you must know it. And knowing it — sitting with the knowledge without letting it break you — is perhaps the most specifically human thing there is. Animals flee pain. Humans carry it, examine it, write letters about it, and still somehow love each other across the distance of it.

"We cannot be free until they are free."

— James Baldwin

Baldwin's letter is not about despair — though it knows despair intimately. It is about the extraordinary act of choosing to love in a world that gives you every reason not to. And it is about the even more extraordinary act of passing that capacity to love, intact and undiminished, to the next person — which is perhaps the whole point of being human, when you strip everything else away.

Topic Two
Beautiful Melancholy
For when sadness feels like poetry and longing has no name.
A note before you read: I picked these because they don't try to fix sadness — they sit with it. They make it feel like something worth having, not something to get rid of. Read these slowly. 🌧️
Essay · Aeon Magazine
Saudade — The Untranslatable Feeling of Longing for What May Never Have Been
Aeon Magazine · Culture & Language

There is a word in Portuguese — saudade — that has no clean equivalent in English, and that very absence says something important about the English-speaking world's discomfort with a particular kind of feeling: the deep, bittersweet longing for something or someone you love but may never have again. Or may never have had at all.

The word appears in the earliest Portuguese poetry, in fado music, in the work of Fernando Pessoa, who described it as "the love that remains after someone is gone." Others define it as nostalgia for what was never yours. The philosopher Agostinho da Silva called it a longing for the ideal — a perfection that reality never quite manages to deliver.

"Saudade is not sadness. It is the memory of feeling, carrying itself forward into a present that cannot contain it."

— Aeon Magazine
✦

Why untranslatable feelings matter

Languages carve up the emotional world differently. When a language has a word for something and another doesn't, it suggests that the speakers of that language noticed something the others didn't. The Japanese have mono no aware — the bittersweet awareness of impermanence. The Germans have sehnsucht — a deep longing for an alternative life, a road not taken.

These words matter because naming something is the first step toward not being consumed by it. When you have a word for a feeling, you know you are not alone in it. The word proves that someone, somewhere, felt this same shapeless ache — felt it so frequently and distinctly that they named it, passed the name on, and now it exists in the language like a small lighthouse in the fog.

"In Portuguese there is a word — saudade — that I keep reaching for in English and finding nothing there. It is the feeling of missing what you love. Even while it is still present. Even if it never quite was."

— adapted from various sources

Perhaps the deepest comfort saudade offers is this: you are allowed to miss things. You are allowed to be moved by what is gone, or by what was never yours. The feeling is not a malfunction. It is proof that you loved something, or tried to. And that, the Portuguese seem to suggest, is enough.

Essay · The School of Life
In Praise of Melancholy — Why Sadness Makes Us More Human
The School of Life · Philosophy

We live in an age that is deeply, pathologically, almost violently optimistic. We are told, in a hundred ways, that happiness is the default human state, that sadness is a problem to be solved. Melancholy, in this framing, is a malfunction. A bug in the system.

But there is another view — far older, and perhaps far wiser — that says melancholy is not a bug at all. It is a feature. It is the mood of the person who has looked at life carefully and seen it clearly, with all its impermanence, its loss, its beauty and its waste. Melancholy is the emotional signature of depth.

"Melancholy is not the same as depression. Depression wants nothing. Melancholy wants everything — and is sad because it cannot have it."

— The School of Life
✦

The intelligence of sadness

The melancholic person is sitting with reality. They are not pretending that loss doesn't hurt, that time doesn't pass, that the things we love don't eventually leave us. They are refusing the comfortable lie. And in that refusal there is a kind of courage that the relentlessly cheerful person never has to summon.

The great art of the world is almost entirely made by melancholics. Music that makes you feel something real. Novels that stay with you. These things come from people who sat with difficulty long enough to understand it. The melancholic person is also, typically, kinder — because they know what it is to be sad, they recognize it in others.

"The goal is not to eliminate melancholy but to let it speak — to hear what it is trying to say about what we love, what we've lost, and what we hope for."

— The School of Life

So if you are melancholic — if you feel things too deeply, notice too much, are moved by things that pass other people by — please understand: this is not a defect. It is a gift. A heavy one, sometimes. But a gift.

Letter · Rainer Maria Rilke · 1903
On Living the Questions — A Letter from Rilke
Rainer Maria Rilke · Letters to a Young Poet, 1903

You are so young; you stand before beginnings. I would like to beg you, dear friend, to have patience with everything unresolved in your heart and try to love the questions themselves as if they were locked rooms or books written in a very foreign language. Don't search for the answers, which could not be given to you now, because you would not be able to live them. And the point is, to live everything. Live the questions now. Perhaps then, someday far in the future, you will gradually, without even noticing it, live your way into the answer.

"I would like to beg you to have patience with everything unresolved in your heart and to try to love the questions themselves."

— Rainer Maria Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet
✦

On sadness as a room that changes you

Perhaps all the dragons in our lives are princesses who are only waiting to see us act, just once, with beauty and courage. Perhaps everything that frightens us is, in its deepest essence, something helpless that wants our love.

You mustn't be frightened if a sadness rises in front of you, larger than any you have ever seen. You must think that something is happening with you, that life has not forgotten you, that it holds you in its hand and will not let you fall. Why do you want to shut out of your life any agitation, any pain, any melancholy, since you really do not know what these states are working upon you?

"The question, always, is not how to stop feeling sad. It is how to let sadness do its slow work, and trust that you will emerge from it knowing something you didn't know before."

— after Rilke

Topic Three
Science & Wonder
When the universe feels too big, too strange, and too beautiful to ignore.
A note before you read: These aren't textbook science — they're the moments when science crashes into awe. The kind of reads that make you stare at the ceiling afterward and think, wait, but actually though. 🔭
Landmark Essay · Eugene Wigner · 1960
The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences
Eugene Wigner · Communications in Pure and Applied Mathematics, 1960

There is a strange and somewhat frightening fact about the universe that most people never think about: mathematics works. Not just approximately — but with a precision that is almost grotesque. The same abstract structures that pure mathematicians invent in their heads — with no interest whatsoever in the physical world — turn out, again and again, to be exactly the right language for describing that world.

"The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve."

— Eugene Wigner, 1960
✦

Why this should astonish you

Complex numbers were invented by mathematicians playing with the square roots of negative numbers — purely abstract, purely recreational. Centuries later, quantum mechanics discovered that complex numbers are not just useful — they are essential. You cannot write down the basic equations of quantum theory without them.

Riemann developed a new geometry in the 1850s, exploring curved spaces, for pure intellectual pleasure. No one thought it had anything to do with physics. Half a century later, Einstein needed exactly Riemann's geometry to describe the curvature of spacetime in general relativity. The fit was perfect.

"It is not at all natural that 'laws of nature' exist, much less that man is able to discover them. The fact that this miracle occurs makes one wonder whether there is some deeper truth behind it all."

— Wigner

There is no answer at the end of this essay. That is what makes it so good. It is one of the rare pieces of scientific writing that is genuinely comfortable not knowing — that treats wonder as a conclusion, not a failure.

📖 ~13 min read For ceiling-staring, universe-pondering evenings 🌌
Philosophy of Mind · Thomas Nagel · 1974
What Is It Like to Be a Bat?
Thomas Nagel · The Philosophical Review, 1974

Bats use echolocation to navigate. They emit high-frequency sounds, and the bouncing echoes give them a rich, detailed picture of their environment. Presumably, there is something it is like to be a bat experiencing echolocation. Some inner quality. Some sensation. But what? And more importantly — can we ever know?

"An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism — some subjective character of experience."

— Thomas Nagel, 1974
✦

The hard problem of consciousness

I can imagine behaving as a bat behaves — hanging upside down, flying, catching insects with my mouth. But that is not the same as imagining what it is like to be a bat. I can imagine, from the outside, what a bat does. But I cannot access — even in imagination — the interior of bat experience. The "what it's like" of being a bat is, by definition, from the bat's point of view. And that point of view is inaccessible to me.

Nagel uses this to make a larger argument: even if we understood every neuron in a bat's brain, even if we mapped every electrical signal and chemical reaction, we would still not have explained the subjective character of bat experience. This is the "hard problem" of consciousness, and it applies to human consciousness too. Explaining why any of it is accompanied by experience — why there is something it is like to be you, reading this sentence, right now — is a problem that science, as currently practiced, doesn't have the tools to solve.

"Read this one slowly. It will change the way you think about thinking."

— a note for Divya

Science Essay · Carl Sagan · 1994
Pale Blue Dot — A Vision of the Human Future in Space
Carl Sagan · Pale Blue Dot, 1994

From this distant vantage point, the Earth might not seem of any particular interest. But for us, it's different. Consider again that dot. That's here. That's home. That's us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives.

The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child — all lived there, on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.

"The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot."

— Carl Sagan, Pale Blue Dot, 1994
✦

What the cosmos says about us

Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves. The Earth is the only world known so far to harbor life. There is nowhere else, at least in the near future, to which our species could migrate.

"There is perhaps no better demonstration of the folly of human conceits than this distant image of our tiny world. To me, it underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another."

— Sagan

Sagan wrote this after seeing a photograph taken of Earth from 6 billion kilometres away — a tiny, pale blue point of light in a ray of scattered sunlight. It remains one of the most beautiful and quietly devastating pieces of scientific writing ever produced.

Topic Four
Fantasy & The Impossible
For dreamers who love doors that stay closed — and imagine what's behind them anyway.
A note before you read: These are for the part of you that builds entire worlds in your head and mourns that you can't visit them. The impossible is worth grieving. It means you imagined something beautiful. ✨
Essay · J.R.R. Tolkien · 1947
On Fairy-Stories — Why Fantasy Is the Most Human Thing We Do
J.R.R. Tolkien · Essays Presented to Charles Williams, 1947

Fantasy is a natural human activity. It certainly does not destroy or even insult Reason; and it does not blunt the appetite for, nor obscure the perception of, scientific verity. On the contrary. The keener and the clearer is the reason, the better fantasy will it make.

Tolkien argues — with the patience and precision of a scholar who has spent his life thinking about this — that human beings are sub-creators. We are made in the image of a Creator, and what we inevitably do, what we cannot stop doing, is create. Stories. Worlds. Myths. The impulse to imagine things that don't exist is not childishness. It is the most distinctly human impulse there is.

"Fantasy remains a human right: we make in our measure and in our derivative mode, because we are made: and not only made, but made in the image and likeness of a Maker."

— J.R.R. Tolkien, On Fairy-Stories
✦

On escape — and why it is not cowardice

The motive of the prisoner is escape. The critics of Fantasy confuse this with the flight of the deserter. They are not the same. Fantasy is not the flight of someone who cannot face reality — it is the cry of someone who has faced it and decided, quite rightly, that reality is not the only thing worth living in.

"The consolation of fairy-stories, the joy of the happy ending ... is not essentially 'escapist'. In its fairy-tale setting, it is a sudden glimpse of the underlying reality or truth."

— Tolkien

Read this and feel entirely, completely justified in every world you've ever built in your head.

📖 ~15 min read For every impossible world you've ever mourned ✨
Science + Wonder · Nautilus Magazine
Many Worlds — How Quantum Physics Makes the Impossible Routine
Nautilus Magazine · Science & Philosophy

In 1957, a graduate student at Princeton named Hugh Everett III proposed something that sounded, at first, like science fiction. Every time a quantum event occurs — every time a particle is measured, every time a choice is made at the quantum level — the universe doesn't pick one outcome. It branches. Every possible outcome happens, in a separate branch of reality. All of them. Simultaneously. Forever.

"The universe is not stranger than we suppose. It is stranger than we can suppose."

— after J.B.S. Haldane
✦

The fantasy that physics might make real

In the Many-Worlds picture, there is a branch of reality where every decision you've ever made went differently. Where you took the other road. Where you said the thing you didn't say. These branches are not accessible to you — but they are, if the theory is correct, real. They exist. They happened.

For those who love fantasy — who have always felt that the worlds they imagine have some quality of reality — this is not just science. It is almost comfort. The worlds you imagine might not be so different from the worlds that physics says exist, out there in the branching structure of the quantum multiverse.

"Reality at its smallest scales is not like anything you have ever seen. It does not follow the rules you grew up with. It is, in the most literal sense, fantastical."

— Nautilus Magazine

The impossible, it turns out, is only impossible at the scale we happen to live at. Zoom in far enough, and everything gets strange. The universe has been running on magic the whole time. We just couldn't see it.

Topic Five
Philosophical Ideas
For minds that won't stop asking "but why?" even when it's 2am.
A note before you read: I chose these because they don't give you answers — they give you better questions. And for someone like you, a really good question is worth more than a hundred easy answers. 🕯️
Philosophy · Albert Camus · 1942
The Myth of Sisyphus — We Must Imagine Sisyphus Happy
Albert Camus · Le Mythe de Sisyphe, 1942

There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide. Judging whether life is or is not worth living amounts to answering the fundamental question of philosophy. Camus begins here — not to shock, but to be honest. If life has no inherent meaning, he asks, why do we bother? And his answer is one of the strangest and most beautiful in all of philosophy: because we must. And because, in the very act of continuing despite meaninglessness, we create something that looks an awful lot like dignity.

"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

— Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, 1942
✦

The boulder, the hill, and the choice

Sisyphus is condemned by the gods to push a boulder up a hill for eternity. Each time he reaches the top, it rolls back down. He descends and begins again. Forever. Camus watches Sisyphus descend the hill — this is the moment that interests him. Because at that moment, Sisyphus sees his condition clearly. He knows there is no point. He knows the boulder will roll down again. He knows this will never end. And Camus says: at that moment, Sisyphus is superior to his fate.

"The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

— Camus

Meaning is not found, it is made. Not given to you by the universe, but asserted against the universe by you. Sisyphus doesn't find a reason to push the boulder. He becomes his own reason. And that, Camus says, is freedom. After you read this, notice how you feel the next time something feels pointless. Notice whether Sisyphus comes to mind.

Essay / Speech · David Foster Wallace · 2005
This Is Water — On Living a Compassionate Life
David Foster Wallace · Kenyon College Commencement Address, 2005

There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says "Morning, boys. How's the water?" And the two young fish swim on for a bit, and then eventually one of them looks over at the other and goes "What the hell is water?"

"The most obvious, ubiquitous, important realities are often the ones that are hardest to see and talk about."

— David Foster Wallace, This Is Water, 2005
✦

The default setting

Here is the thing: there is no such thing as not worshipping. Everybody worships. The only choice we get is what to worship. If you worship money and things, you will never have enough. Worship your body and you will always feel ugly. Worship power and you will end up feeling weak and afraid. The default setting of the human mind is self-centredness — not a moral failing, but a biological fact. The cars in the traffic jam are obstacles in my commute. The people in the queue are in my way.

"Learning how to think really means learning how to exercise some control over how and what you think. It means being conscious enough to choose what you pay attention to."

— Wallace

But you can choose differently. You can, in the moment of most automatic reaction, pause. You can imagine that the slow driver in front of you has just come from the hospital. You can choose to see the person, not the obstacle. This is what Wallace calls "This Is Water." This — the conscious, daily effort to be present and kind and aware — is what it means to be educated. Not the degree. Not the facts. This. Reads in fifteen minutes. Lives in you for decades.

Philosophy · Simone de Beauvoir · 1947
On Ambiguity — Why Freedom Is Frightening and Necessary
Simone de Beauvoir · The Ethics of Ambiguity, 1947

De Beauvoir begins with something that sounds small and becomes enormous: the problem of wanting things. Of being moved by beauty and not knowing what to do with that. Of desiring something larger than yourself and not knowing how to reach it. This, she says, is the fundamental condition of human freedom — and it is terrifying.

"We are condemned to be free. And freedom is not a gift — it is a burden we must learn to carry with grace."

— after Simone de Beauvoir
✦

The ethics of existing ambiguously

De Beauvoir's great contribution is this: she takes the existentialist idea that existence has no inherent meaning, and instead of despairing, she asks what we owe each other in a meaningless world. And her answer is: everything. My freedom, she argues, is only real if yours is too. A freedom built on the oppression of others is not freedom — it is just another form of hiding from the responsibility of being free.

"To will oneself free is also to will others free."

— Simone de Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity

The ambiguity of the title is this: human beings are both subjects and objects. Both free and constrained. Both reaching for the infinite and limited by the finite. De Beauvoir says: don't resolve this tension. Live in it. The ambiguity is not a problem. It is the condition. For a mind that won't stop asking "but why?" — this is the place to start.
